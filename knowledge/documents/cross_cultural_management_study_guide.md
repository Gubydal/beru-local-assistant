1. Globalization & international business
Source: DOC Support 1-1, pp. 1–12 (Fatehi, Managing Internationally, pp. 7–17)
Central idea: The global business environment changes the way multinational companies (MNCs) compete.
International managers must understand both business conditions and cultural differences.
The changing international environment: 11 drivers
Decreasing trade barriers: fewer tariffs and restrictions make cross-border trade easier. The chapter
mentions GATT, NAFTA and regional free trade arrangements.
Developing countries' changing attitude to MNCs and FDI: foreign direct investment becomes
attractive for technology, jobs, exports and development; governments may still seek to protect national
interests.
Export-oriented strategies: firms and countries increasingly target foreign customers, rather than only
domestic markets.
Spread of regional trade agreements: economic blocs change market access, competition and where
MNCs invest or operate.
Technological development: manufacturing advances, robotics, flexible production, CAD/CAM and
information technologies reshape costs and international competition.
Increasing demand for capital: large investment needs push countries and firms toward international
financing and capital markets.
Diminishing effectiveness of national borders: cross-border people, money, products and management
practices are increasingly interconnected, even though governments and borders still matter.
FDI investment requirements: expensive R&D, machinery and new technology push companies to
access multiple markets, expertise and funding sources.
Increasing interdependency among nations: an economic or political change in one country can affect
firms and customers in another.
The advent of the Internet: firms can research customers, coordinate supply chains, communicate and
sell internationally more efficiently.

Three phases of international linkage
The text distinguishes earlier cross-border trade, then broader investment/production connections, and a
more integrated globalization phase involving capital, goods, services, knowledge, technology and labor. The
last phase means competition and coordination occur across borders rather than simply through exports.

From the last pages of the excerpt: the Internet can improve customer reach, support central or regional

Systematic and organized: values, norms, institutions and behaviors are linked; changing one element

3. Guy Rocher: culture, civilization, functions & instinct
Source: DOC Support 2-2, Guy Rocher, La notion de culture, pp. 1–10
History of the concept
In common French, culture once referred to cultivating land; in modern anthropology and sociology it came to
mean a society's learned way of life. Edward B. Tylor made the anthropological definition famous in 1871 in 
Primitive Culture. Anthropology used the concept earlier than many traditions of sociology. American
researchers helped spread the term; it entered French sociology more widely later.
Culture versus civilization
Rocher explains that scholars have not always drawn the same boundary. One historical distinction assigns
material/technical means to one term and intellectual/spiritual activity to the other; another reverses that
allocation. Because this split is ambiguous, many sociologists and anthropologists use culture and civilization
interchangeably.
Two more specific uses of “civilization”: (1) a larger family of related cultures extending across space/time;
(2) a society described as reaching advanced scientific, technical or urban development. The second use can
carry value judgments, so social science often prefers narrower terms such as industrialization, development
or modernization.
Rocher's precise definition, unpacked
Thinking, feeling and acting: culture affects cognitive, emotional and behavioral life.
More or less formalized: some rules are explicit (laws, protocols); others are tacit (manners, social
expectations).

Instinct versus culture (Rocher's final pages)
Instinct is described in biological discussions as largely innate/inherited behavior, whereas culture is learned,
social and accumulated across generations. Both help living beings respond to their environments, but human
symbolic ability allows cultural knowledge to be transmitted and transformed. Do not confuse this comparison
with a claim that all behavior is purely cultural.
Memorize: Rocher = thinking + feeling + acting; learned + shared; objective + symbolic; social
function + psychic function.

5. The major cross-cultural models
Source: DOC Support 3-2, pp. 1–7
Why compare cultures?
Frameworks help identify cultural distance and differences in typical values, communication and interaction.
They are analytical tools—not precise personality tests or instructions to stereotype people.

Alexander Thomas — cultural standards
A qualitative method using interviews about critical incidents or unexpectedly difficult intercultural situations. If
many members of a group repeatedly respond in similar ways, researchers identify a possible cultural
standard. Key difference: the method starts with real incidents, not just numeric national-dimension scores.
Kluckhohn & Strodtbeck — six value orientations (1961)
Question Orientations in the support
What is human nature? Good / evil / mixed
How should humans relate to nature? Domination / subjugation / harmony
How should people relate to others? Individualist / group / hierarchical
What human activity matters? Being / achieving / doing
Which time matters? Past / present / future
How is space understood? Private / public / mixed (added later)
Edward T. Hall — three concepts
High-context communication
Meaning depends more on relationship, shared
knowledge, tone, non-verbal signals and
situation. Some messages are indirect.
Low-context communication
Meaning is communicated more directly and
explicitly through words, instructions and clear
contracts.
Monochronic time: sequential tasks, schedules and doing one thing at a time. Polychronic time: multiple
tasks/relationships overlap; schedules may be handled more flexibly. Proxemics: how people use
interpersonal distance and physical space. These are contrasts on continua, not fixed country labels.
Edgar Schein — the three levels of organizational culture
Visible artifacts
Dress, logos, office layout, procedures, stories, symbols
Espoused values
Declared principles, priorities, statements of what matters
Underlying assumptions
Deep, often unconscious beliefs taken for granted
Exam trap: artifacts are easy to observe but not always easy to interpret. Underlying assumptions are deepest
and hardest to change.

Trompenaars & Hampden-Turner — seven orientations
Universalism / particularism: general rules versus special relationships and circumstances.
Individualism / communitarianism: individual welfare and achievement versus the wider group.
Specific / diffuse: separation of private/professional life versus overlap.
Neutral / affective: controlled display of feelings versus open emotional expression.
Achievement / ascription: status earned by results versus status attached to position, background or
other attributes.
Sequential / synchronous time: time as ordered steps versus parallel/interconnected processes.
Inner / outer direction: controlling surroundings versus adapting to external conditions.
The GLOBE project
Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness. It studies leadership across cultures. The
support asks whether and how leadership principles can be applied globally. Its central logic: societal culture
predicts organizational practices, and what leadership behaviors are accepted/expected differs by cultural
environment. The support does not enumerate a complete set of GLOBE scores or culture clusters.
Cultural Orientations Indicator (COI)
Developed by D. Walker, T. Walker and J. Schmitz. An 87-question tool for work-related cultural
preferences, combining 10 dimensions, 17 continua and 36 orientations (as the support states). It explores
thinking style, interaction style and sense of self.

6. Organizational culture: definitions, roles & models
Source: DOC Support 5-1, pp. 1–7
What is organizational (corporate) culture?
The shared values, meanings, assumptions, rules and patterns that guide how people behave within an
organization. The support presents a debate: culture is something an organization is (deep, relatively
stable identity) versus something an organization has (can be managed and changed as environments
evolve).
Definitions in the support
Goffee & Jones: culture helps hold the organization together.
Meyerson & Martin: culture acts as social/normative “glue” across different group members.
Pedersen & Sorensen: organizations create systems of meanings shaping behavior, routines and
practices.
Camerer & Vepsäläinen: broad tacit rules tell employees what to do even in situations that cannot be
anticipated.
French et al.: shared values and beliefs guiding behavior.
Conners: shared patterns of beliefs, behavior and assumptions acquired over time.
Influences: national cultures, differences inside the organization, differences between firms, and the
organization's own history. A multinational company can develop a common corporate culture that crosses
national borders.

Why culture matters
Culture creates bonds, helps people act when rules are incomplete, influences information handling, supports
coordination and control, shapes conflict resolution, reduces uncertainty, strengthens motivation/cohesion, and
can contribute to competitive advantage. The support refers to research by Cameron & Quinn and Trice &
Beyer linking culture to organizational performance, longevity, morale, commitment, productivity and well-
being. Association is not proof that culture alone causes success.

9. Altrad Group: applied intercultural management
Source: CAS Cultural Differences – Altrad Group, pp. 1–13
The central managerial question
As Altrad expands internationally, particularly through acquisitions beginning in 1997, it encounters different
national, regional, professional and organizational cultures. Which strategy creates cohesion without
destroying local identities?
Five strategies facing cultural diversity

2. Deny differences Impose a single dominant language, rules,
products or culture.
Rejected: resentment, demotivation and
weakened communication.

Important detail: the source lists five attitudes and calls valorization the final desired approach. One
paragraph loosely refers to it as a “fourth strategy”; retain the actual five-item classification.
How the preferred strategy works
Altrad aims to create a common “Altrad spirit” that respects national, linguistic and professional identities.
Imagine a cement mixer: different ingredients combine to form a useful whole. Or scaffolding: different
elements provide strength, flexibility and safety through complementary functions. The lesson is unity without
forced uniformity.
Global organizational culture and subcultures
Altrad defines its own culture as activities, operating rules, shared values and history connecting people across
the group. It includes different subcultures: country/region, profession/hierarchical level, company/sector,
product/business activity and clientele. For example, sales staff in different subsidiaries may share
professional norms more strongly than a salesperson and a production worker in the same subsidiary.

## Page 16

Risk: uncontrolled assimilation or externally imposed values can create rejection, confusion of identity or loss
of local cultural meaning. These risks are particularly relevant to mergers and acquisitions.
Management after acquisitions
Altrad emphasizes positive integration and use of existing human, material, financial and cultural capabilities.
The case notes that former company managers often remain in place. Local culture should be preserved and
should also enrich the evolving group-wide culture.
From commercial culture toward financial culture
Altrad historically focused on sales, turnover, market share, clients and contracts. It also needs a stronger
common financial language around margins, receivables, payment terms, working capital and operating
profit/turnover (OP/TO). Different understandings of accounting terms caused conflict, so a committee
created a multilingual financial glossary with consistent definitions and formulas (for example gross profit,
margins, transfer pricing and whether transport costs are included).
Budgetary control as a shared culture
“One man, one budget”: each budget has an accountable owner with authority and responsibility.
Annual planning: forecast sales, income, costs, margins and operating results, compare with current
results and clarify priorities.
Different budget needs: industrial, commercial and service/rental companies use relevant functional
budgets (sales, production, supplies, staff, cash, investments, etc.).
Monthly monitoring: compare forecasts versus actuals and take corrective action, e.g. commercial
response to a sales shortfall or savings after excess spending.
Four-month and year-end reviews: review interim accounts and final financial documents, but also
human, logistical, managerial and strategic aspects.
Participatory coordination: group management sets direction; local managers and staff contribute to
plans and discuss options; the group ultimately validates projects.
The case repeatedly warns that figures are signs and consequences, not the entirety of business reality.
Real culture combines common methods and shared responsibility with local autonomy, trust and personal
involvement.
Model answer to “What is Altrad's strategy?”: It valorizes cultural differences rather than erasing
them, builds a common group identity from local subcultures, and uses shared language and
participative budget systems to produce cohesion and performance.

## Page 17

Question Correct distinction
Is culture only the arts? No—this is the narrow everyday meaning; anthropology and
sociology use broader definitions.
Culture versus personality? Culture is shared and learned by groups; personality is particular
to a person.
Culture versus instinct? Culture is learned/transmitted socially; instinct is presented as
innate/biological in Rocher's comparison.
Hall versus Hofstede? Hall: communication context, time, space. Hofstede: six national
cultural dimensions.
Schein versus Trompenaars? Schein: layers of an organization's culture. Trompenaars: seven
national dilemmas and four corporate culture types in the
corporate culture handout.
High uncertainty avoidance? More discomfort with unclear or unfamiliar conditions, not
necessarily “more risk” in every sense.
High power distance? Greater acceptance of hierarchical inequality; it does not mean
every individual is obedient.
High masculinity score? More weight on achievement and competition, not a simple count
of men or women.
Individualism versus selfishness? Individualism describes a cultural emphasis on autonomy and
identity; it is not a moral judgment.
Generation stereotype versus evidence? A cohort description or employer opinion cannot prove that every
person in that cohort behaves alike.
Altrad adaptation versus valorization? Adaptation accommodates differences; valorization deliberately
uses them to create shared value.
Useful short-answer structure
For a definition question: define → name author → explain in plain language → give example. For a case
question: identify problem → select relevant model → explain observed behavior → recommend action
and limitation. For survey interpretation: state sample and group → quote the relevant scores → explain
the dimension → note data limitations.

## Page 22

Answer key with explanations
1. A — Edward Hall. Hall connects communication context with culture; he also studied time and proxemics.
2. B — Shared and learned meanings, practices and values. Anthropological culture includes learned beliefs,
symbols, norms, work, language and social life.
3. D — Removing all budgets. Altrad compares ignorance, denial, exclusion, adaptation and valorization.
4. C — Valuing cultural differences. Altrad treats differences as resources rather than obstacles.
5. A — Acceptance of unequal power. PDI = Power Distance Index.
6. B — Discomfort with uncertainty. UAI = Uncertainty Avoidance Index.
7. C — Underlying assumptions. Basic assumptions are taken for granted and often unconscious.
8. A — The study of personal space and distance. Hall studied how space and distance are culturally patterned.
9. C — Eiffel Tower. Eiffel Tower = high formalization and high centralization.
10. A — Guided Missile. Guided Missile emphasizes tasks, teams and achievement.
11. B — Binding individuals into a collective. Social culture creates belonging and solidarity.
12. A — Shaping ways of thinking and feeling. Culture acts as a flexible mold of personality.
13. B — VSM-13. The source names the VSM-13 questionnaire.
14. C — 321. 193 students + 128 parents = 321; nearly 900 refers to the three-year study.
15. A — Individualism. IDV: 93.8 in students versus 49.4 in parents.
16. C — UAI. The UAI overview conflicts with its reported gender breakdown.
17. B — More freedom to enjoy leisure and gratification. IVR contrasts indulgence with restraint.
18. A — Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner. It contrasts consistent rules with exceptions/relationships.
19. A — Leadership across cultures. GLOBE is Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness.
20. B — Critical incidents and qualitative interviews. Recurring reactions in critical intercultural incidents are
examined.
21. B — Digital natives. Gen Z is described as highly connected digital natives.
22. B — Misunderstanding of financial definitions. Shared terminology can reduce unproductive disputes about
margins and other formulas.
23. A — Max Weber. Weber links religious orientations with capitalist development.
24. D — 10. The support lists ten COI dimensions.
25. B — They offer group-level frameworks, not certainties about individuals. Avoid stereotyping; check the
individual, group and situation.
